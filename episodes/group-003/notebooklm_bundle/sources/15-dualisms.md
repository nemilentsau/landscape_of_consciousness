# Section 15: Dualisms

## Kuhn Review Anchor
- Section: 15. Dualisms
- Pages: 83-83
- Taxonomy placement: Dualisms

## Research Question
If consciousness feels radically unlike brain tissue, does that mean reality needs a second kind of thing, or only that our physical explanation is incomplete?

## Core Claim
Kuhn's section 15 introduces dualism as the broad family of consciousness theories that posit at least two ontological categories: the physical brain and something mental or soul-like that is not identical with the physical world as currently conceived. In ordinary usage this usually means substance dualism: a person is, or includes essentially, a nonphysical mind or soul distinct from the body. Kuhn correctly separates this from property dualism, which may posit irreducible phenomenal properties without a separable soul. The key pressure point is causal: if the nonphysical mind affects speech, action, attention, or memory, then the physical world is not causally closed in the way many physicalists assume. If it does not affect the physical world, dualism risks epiphenomenalism, where consciousness is real but causally idle. The section should therefore frame dualism less as one doctrine than as a decision tree: Cartesian substance dualism, soul-centered religious dualisms, hylomorphic and emergent dualisms, interactionism, epiphenomenalism, parallelism, and naturalistic dualism all preserve a mental/physical divide in different ways.

## Strongest Case
The strongest case for dualism is not that neuroscience has failed, but that third-person physical structure still seems to leave out first-person phenomenal character. Descartes gives the classical substance-dualist template: mind is essentially thinking and non-extended, body is extended and non-thinking, so they appear to be distinct kinds of substance. Plato's Phaedo supplies an older soul-centered version, tying personhood to a soul whose fate is not simply the body's fate. Contemporary arguments update the pressure. Chalmers's epistemic-gap framework argues that complete physical and functional facts do not transparently entail the existence of experience; Mary, zombies, Nagel's bat, and Levine's explanatory gap all push in the same direction. Swinburne's modern substance dualism adds a personal-identity argument: first-person identity and co-consciousness seem not to be captured by descriptions of a brain's parts, and it seems at least conceivable that the same subject could survive radical bodily or neural replacement. Interactionism also has a commonsense advantage: pain seems to make one withdraw, intentions seem to move bodies, and moral agency seems to require mental states to matter causally. A charitable formulation says dualism is trying to preserve three data at once: the reality of subjective experience, the unity and persistence of the subject, and the apparent causal role of deliberation and experience.

## Best Objections And Limits
The best objections are cumulative. First is the interaction problem, sharpened by Princess Elisabeth's challenge to Descartes: if a soul is non-extended and not in physical contact with the body, what is the mind-body causal nexus? Contemporary versions appeal to conservation laws, the pairing problem, and the completeness or causal closure of the physical: if every bodily motion has a sufficient physical cause, a distinct nonphysical cause looks either excluded or systematically overdetermining. Second, dualism is explanatorily expensive. It adds souls, psychophysical laws, or nonphysical substances without a worked-out mechanism for when they arise, how they attach to particular organisms, how they vary across species and development, or why brain injury, anesthesia, sleep, drugs, stimulation, and neurodegeneration alter consciousness so reliably. Third, dualism can trade one mystery for two: it leaves the hard problem in place and adds the problem of how an immaterial subject knows, unifies, remembers, and causes anything. Fourth, epiphenomenalist versions preserve causal closure but make experience causally irrelevant to reports about experience, which threatens knowledge of consciousness itself. Fifth, Kuhn's survey gloss should be handled carefully: PhilPapers 2020 all-respondent figures show physicalism at 51.93% and non-physicalism at 32.08%, but PhilPapers warns these should not be used as a simple longitudinal comparison with 2009; the comparable-department analysis shows physicalism roughly stable or slightly higher. Finally, soul-centered versions often depend on theological or spiritual premises. Those may be important culturally, but they are not independently confirmed by current neuroscience or physics.

## Credibility / Epistemic Status
Epistemic status: active philosophical debate for dualism as a family of mind-body positions; religious/spiritual metaphysics for immortal-soul versions tied to God, reincarnation, afterlife, or cosmic planes; speculative extension for naturalistic interactionist or property-dualist laws; fringe/weakly evidenced claim when presented as empirically proven survival after death or paranormal causation. Substance dualism is historically central and still defended by serious philosophers, but it is a minority view in contemporary academic philosophy and not a mainstream scientific theory. Neuroscience strongly supports dependence of ordinary human consciousness on brain state, while philosophical arguments from phenomenal character, conceivability, and personal identity keep nonphysicalist positions alive. The responsible course framing is: dualism is intellectually serious as a metaphysical response to the hard problem, culturally pervasive through soul traditions, but scientifically underdeveloped and under heavy pressure from causal closure, parsimony, and brain-dependence evidence.
