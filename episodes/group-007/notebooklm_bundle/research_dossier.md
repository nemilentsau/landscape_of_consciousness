## Episode Scope And Why These Sections Are Grouped

This dossier covers five subfamilies at the front of Kuhn's materialist cluster. They are grouped because all reject a nonphysical soul or supernatural agency as the required explanatory basis of ordinary consciousness, while disagreeing about the level at which consciousness should be located.

Philosophical materialism provides the conceptual machinery: physicalism, identity theory, functionalism, eliminative materialism, illusionism, emergence, epiphenomenalism, and debates about what the physical means. Neurobiological theories then move into brain mechanisms: neural correlates, recurrent and global circuits, thalamo-cortical dynamics, attention, perturbational complexity, and clinical evidence. Electromagnetic field theories propose a different physical substrate or control variable inside the brain: structured electric and magnetic fields generated by neural currents. Computational and informational theories treat consciousness as depending on causal-information organization, computation, resonance, criticality, or architecture. Homeostatic and affective theories foreground living regulation, interoception, allostasis, active inference, valence, and feeling.

The grouping should not make these views look identical. A strict identity theorist, a global-workspace theorist, McFadden's CEMI theory, a Chalmers-style organizational-invariance view, and Damasio's homeostatic-feelings view make different commitments. Their shared materialist bet is narrower: consciousness is to be explained within physical nature through bodies, brains, fields, functions, information processing, or biological regulation rather than by a separately existing mental substance.

The episode question asks for the strongest case and where it breaks. The strongest case is cumulative: interventions on brain and body alter experience; physical explanations avoid competition with causal closure; neuroscience and cognitive science generate testable models; and materialist theories can be compared by explicit mechanisms. The break appears where target phenomena drift, correlations are treated as explanations, formal or neural vocabulary outruns evidence, and phenomenal character remains underexplained.

## Concise Thesis Of The Cluster

Materialism's strongest case is that ordinary human consciousness is deeply and systematically dependent on physical organization. Anesthesia can remove the world of experience while the organism remains alive. Sleep, coma, seizures, stimulation, lesions, drugs, neuromodulation, interoceptive disturbance, and mood disorders can alter level, content, selfhood, affect, agency, and report. Cognitive science and neuroscience can manipulate and measure many of these changes in controlled ways. Philosophical physicalism adds a parsimonious ontology: if behavior and thought unfold in a causally closed physical world, the most economical view is that conscious events are themselves physical events, physical realizations, or physical-functional organizations.

The cluster breaks at the point where dependence is asked to become explanation. A neural correlate can track consciousness without constituting it. A computation can model access or control without guaranteeing phenomenality. An electromagnetic field can be causally relevant without being the bearer of experience. Homeostatic regulation can make value biologically intelligible without showing why need or control feels like pain, hunger, relief, anxiety, or presence. The mature materialist case is not refuted by these gaps, but it remains incomplete unless each theory specifies its target phenomenon, bridge relation, necessity and sufficiency claims, scope limits, and risky tests.

## Per-Section Factual Summaries

### 9.1 Philosophical Theories

Kuhn's section 9.1 is not a lab mechanism. It is the conceptual foundation for materialist consciousness theories. It asks what physicalism means, what mind-brain identity would require, whether mental kinds are functional kinds, whether folk-psychological categories should be reduced or eliminated, whether consciousness can be emergent without becoming nonphysical, and whether materialism can answer anti-physicalist challenges.

The common claim is that consciousness should be understood without positing a nonphysical substance. Type identity theory, associated with Place and Smart, holds that conscious states can be identical with brain states even if mental and neural descriptions differ in meaning. Functionalism broadens the picture: a mental state is identified by its causal role rather than by a single neural type, allowing for multiple realizability across humans, other animals, and possible machines. Eliminative materialism, associated with Churchland and later discussions in the SEP literature, argues that some everyday mental categories may be scientifically replaced rather than reduced. Illusionism and related deflationary views argue that phenomenal consciousness as ordinarily conceived may be a misleading introspective posit.

The strongest philosophical case combines empirical dependence, causal discipline, and metaphysical economy. Ordinary consciousness is altered or removed by physical interventions such as anesthesia, lesions, stimulation, sleep, drugs, seizures, and neurodegeneration. If physical events are causally closed and conscious intentions participate in behavior, the cleanest materialist account is that conscious events are themselves physical events or physical realizations. Papineau-style arguments press this causal-closure point: adding nonphysical causes threatens either causal overdetermination or causal exclusion.

The evidence here is not that philosophy has solved consciousness. The evidence is that materialist philosophy supplies a framework in which neuroscience, psychology, cognitive science, and AI research can seek mechanisms without multiplying ontological kinds. The 2020 PhilPapers Survey shows physicalism is a leading professional view but not a consensus: the research record reports 51.93 percent of respondents accepting or leaning toward physicalism about mind and 32.08 percent accepting or leaning toward nonphysicalism.

The strongest objections are conceptual. Chalmers's hard problem and Levine's explanatory gap ask why any physical or functional process should feel like something from the inside. Jackson's Mary argument presses whether complete physical knowledge leaves out knowledge of experience. Nagel's bat argument stresses perspectival subjectivity. Zombie arguments challenge the move from physical duplication to guaranteed experience. Multiple realizability pressures type identity, while absent and inverted qualia pressure functionalism. Eliminative and illusionist accounts risk denying or redescribing the datum they were meant to explain. Stoljar's physicalism literature also keeps alive the Hempel-style dilemma: define physicalism by current physics and it may be incomplete; define it by future ideal physics and the thesis can become under-specified.

Epistemic status: mainstream scientific anchor for brain dependence; active philosophical debate for the stronger metaphysical claim that consciousness is wholly physical, reducible, identical, functionally realized, or eliminatively revisable.

### 9.2 Neurobiological Theories

Kuhn's section 9.2 groups theories that start from brain mechanisms rather than abstract physicalism alone. These theories treat conscious states as depending on, realized by, caused by, or constituted by specific neurobiological organization: neural transmission, recurrent and long-range circuits, thalamo-cortical and cortico-cortical pathways, electrical dynamics, connectome organization, attention, global availability, and the neural correlates of conscious contents and conscious level.

This is not one theory. The family includes Edelman's reentry and dynamic core, Crick and Koch's NCC program, Baars and Dehaene's global workspace and global neuronal workspace, Dennett's multiple drafts model, Minsky's society of mind, Graziano's attention schema theory, Prinz's attention-based neurofunctionalism, Northoff's temporo-spatial approach, Bunge's emergent materialism, and clinical or perturbational approaches to conscious level. Nearby theories include recurrent-processing accounts, posterior-hot-zone proposals, higher-order theories, predictive processing, and IIT, though Kuhn's larger taxonomy separates some of them elsewhere.

The strongest case is empirical and mechanistic. Consciousness changes with neural injury, anesthesia, sleep, seizures, stimulation, neuromodulation, and disorders of consciousness. Masking and binocular rivalry let researchers compare similar sensory inputs with different awareness. No-report paradigms try to separate conscious perception from the act of reporting. TMS-EEG studies show that wakeful cortex can sustain complex, distributed causal responses while deep sleep or anesthesia can collapse responses into local activity. Casali et al.'s perturbational complexity index is important because it tries to index conscious level independently of ordinary sensory processing and overt behavior.

Major reviews by Koch, Massimini, Boly, and Tononi, and by Seth and Bayne, show real progress but not closure. The field now has finer distinctions among conscious level, conscious contents, report, access, attention, metacognition, and selfhood. The 2025 Cogitate adversarial collaboration raises the standard by testing predictions associated with global neuronal workspace theory and integrated information theory under shared conditions rather than only through post hoc interpretation.

The strongest objection is that neural dependence is not yet constitutive explanation. A signal can track consciousness, enable consciousness, precede consciousness, follow consciousness, or support report and memory without being what makes experience feel like anything. Front-parietal ignition, local sensory recurrence, posterior hot zones, higher-order monitoring, attention schema, temporo-spatial dynamics, and integrated information can reinterpret overlapping data. Reports, confidence ratings, attention, task demands, and working memory can contaminate supposed NCCs. No-report paradigms reduce some confounds but introduce inference problems of their own.

Epistemic status: mainstream scientific research family for brain mechanisms and NCCs; active debate over necessity, sufficiency, and whether any current neural theory explains phenomenal consciousness rather than conditions, contents, access, or report.

### 9.3 Electromagnetic Field Theories

Kuhn's section 9.3 moves the candidate substrate from discrete neurons, synapses, or spike trains to structured electromagnetic fields generated by coordinated neural currents. The conservative claim is mainstream neuroscience: brains generate measurable extracellular electric and magnetic fields; EEG, MEG, ECoG, and LFP signals are windows into these currents; and endogenous-scale fields can weakly influence neural excitability under some conditions. The stronger consciousness claim is that specific field patterns are not merely readouts but the vehicle, substrate, or identity basis of conscious experience.

McFadden's CEMI theory treats conscious contents as causally active information integrated in the brain's global electromagnetic field. Pockett identifies consciousness with certain spatiotemporal field patterns. Jones reviews electromagnetic-field theories of mind. Fingelkurts' operational architectonics links phenomenal structure to nested, transient operational modules. Other field or resonance accounts attempt to explain binding and unity by appealing to continuous physical fields.

The strongest case is a convergence argument. Brain fields are real physical structures, not arbitrary diagrams imposed by scientists. Buzsaki, Anastassiou, and Koch review how EEG, ECoG, LFPs, and spikes arise from extracellular currents. Frohlich and McCormick found that weak electric fields can guide neocortical network activity in vitro. Anastassiou and colleagues reported ephaptic coupling, in which small extracellular fields can entrain cortical neuronal spiking under some conditions. Rodriguez et al. linked long-distance synchronization to perceptual moments. Pinotsis and Miller report stable electric fields in working memory despite representational drift. Xu and colleagues describe large-scale spiral waves related to cognitive processing.

The careful steelman is not that gamma synchrony equals consciousness or that EEG is the mind. It is that field organization may be a causal, spatially integrated control variable whose perturbation should change conscious contents even when ordinary spike-level descriptions look similar. Field theorists also argue that a continuous medium looks promising for binding and unity, because one physical field can integrate distributed neural activity.

The strongest objections are severe. Field efficacy is not field identity. Evidence that fields modulate neurons supports ephaptic coupling and field-level neurodynamics, but it does not establish that the field is the bearer of phenomenal consciousness. Measurement confounds are serious because EEG, MEG, ECoG, and LFP signals are produced by the same transmembrane currents studied by neuron-and-synapse accounts. A field correlate may be a macro-level readout of underlying circuit mechanisms. Specificity is underdeveloped: field theories need rules mapping field geometries, frequencies, amplitudes, boundaries, and temporal patterns to conscious contents and distinguishing conscious from nonconscious field patterns. Generalized seizures also pressure crude synchrony claims, since massive synchrony can accompany impaired consciousness.

Epistemic status: mainstream science for field measurement and limited ephaptic influence; speculative extension for field identity theories of consciousness; weakly evidenced when EM theories are stretched toward survival, paranormal effects, or cosmic consciousness.

### 9.4 Computational and Informational Theories

Kuhn's section 9.4 treats consciousness as explained, realized, or modeled by advanced computational organization and information dynamics. The shared wager is not that every calculation is conscious. It is that consciousness depends on physically implemented causal-information organization: how signals are transformed, integrated, broadcast, stabilized, predicted, resonated, and used for flexible control.

The family includes symbolic and connectionist computational theories, cognitive architectures, neural-network models, Grossberg's Adaptive Resonance Theory, complex adaptive systems, critical-brain models, informational realism and emergent information approaches, holonomic or frequency-domain proposals, and broader mathematical theories. It overlaps with global workspace, predictive processing, IIT, AI consciousness, and computational neuroscience, though those can become separate theory families when specified more tightly.

The strongest case is that computation and information provide a common mechanistic language across brain science, cognitive science, behavior, and artificial systems. Turing made intelligent behavior a target for machine analysis. Putnam and functionalists made multiple realizability central. Fodor and Marr gave influential accounts of cognition as representation and computation at levels beyond raw implementation. Chalmers defended the idea that cognition may be captured by organizational invariants. Neuroscience then gave the empirical version: sensory systems carry information, conscious and unconscious processing differ in access and recurrence, and theories of report, metacognition, attention, and control can be formalized.

Grossberg's Adaptive Resonance Theory links consciousness to resonant states that stabilize attention and learning. Criticality research links conscious states to near-critical cortical dynamics that may support flexible information flow. AI-consciousness indicator work by Butlin and colleagues translates major scientific theories into architecture-level checks, such as recurrence, global availability, higher-order monitoring, and agency-like control. This is a serious methodological contribution even though it does not prove current AI systems are conscious.

The strongest objections are the familiar functionalist and computational objections sharpened for consciousness. Computation may explain access, report, discrimination, learning, and control without explaining why any of those operations feel like anything. Searle's Chinese Room presses the syntax-versus-semantics challenge. Block's absent-qualia and China-brain worries ask whether functional equivalence secures experience. If computation is defined too broadly, many physical systems implement some computation; if information is Shannon information alone, it tracks uncertainty reduction without supplying subjectivity, meaning, or value. Piccinini and Scarantino's distinction between computation and information processing is important because the two slogans are not interchangeable.

Biological and embodied critics add that metabolism, affect, interoception, development, sensorimotor coupling, analog timing, and living self-maintenance may be constitutive rather than optional. AI and uploading claims are therefore high-risk extrapolations unless tied to explicit criteria.

Epistemic status: mainstream as a modeling framework in cognitive science, neuroscience, computational psychiatry, and AI; active philosophical debate as a sufficient account of phenomenal consciousness; speculative extension for strong claims about AI consciousness or digital immortality.

### 9.5 Homeostatic and Affective Theories

Kuhn's section 9.5 approaches consciousness through predictive regulation of a living organism. The family foregrounds homeostasis, allostasis, interoception, free-energy or active-inference control, equilibrium, affect, and bodily selfhood. Its shared wager is that experience is not neutral cognition with feeling added later. Experience arises in a body-brain system that must keep itself viable, anticipate threats and opportunities, assign value, and regulate action.

Damasio emphasizes homeostatic feelings and the biology of consciousness. Seth develops interoceptive inference and controlled-perception approaches to self and world. Barrett and Simmons emphasize interoceptive predictions and allostasis. Friston supplies the free-energy principle and active inference as a formal framework for perception and action. Solms and Panksepp foreground affective systems and the subjective urgency of needs. LeDoux and Brown offer a competing higher-order theory of emotional consciousness, showing that affect-first theories remain contested.

The strongest case is biological and phenomenological. Consciousness is not only object recognition. Objects matter to organisms as dangerous, inviting, disgusting, needed, painful, relieving, mine, or urgent. Air hunger, pain, nausea, panic, hunger, fatigue, fever, mood, psychedelics, depersonalization, and bodily threat can reorganize the conscious field. Interoceptive anatomy gives plausible channels through brainstem, hypothalamic, insular, cingulate, and visceromotor systems. Kleckner and colleagues identify large-scale allostatic-interoceptive networks in humans. Predictive processing and active inference offer a formal story in which perception, action, self-modeling, affect, and bodily budgeting are coordinated parts of one control architecture.

The strongest objection is overbreadth. Homeostasis is everywhere in biology and regulatory control appears in thermostats, Watt governors, bacteria, plants, immune systems, and unconscious autonomic loops. If a theory does not specify what extra structure turns regulation into feeling, it risks making too much conscious. Many homeostatic processes are unconscious, and many conscious contents, such as geometry, music, mathematical thought, or silent imagery, do not present primarily as bodily need states. Competing theories can accept interoception and affect as important without granting them foundational status.

Free-energy and active-inference theories face an additional worry. In very general form, FEP can become a redescriptive principle rather than a discriminating mechanism. Williams and other critics ask whether the brain is literally an organ for free-energy minimization or whether the framework needs extra process-level assumptions to generate testable predictions. LeDoux and Brown press another targeted challenge: survival circuits and defensive responses are not the same as conscious fear, and emotional consciousness may require higher-order cortical representation.

Epistemic status: mainstream science for the importance of homeostasis, allostasis, interoception, affect, prediction, and bodily regulation in animal consciousness and emotion; active debate for the stronger claim that these mechanisms identify sufficient conditions for phenomenal consciousness.

## Strongest Academic Case For The Cluster

The strongest academic case for this materialist cluster has five linked pillars.

First, ordinary human consciousness is intervention-sensitive in ways that strongly constrain any theory. Anesthesia, sleep, coma, seizures, lesions, stimulation, drugs, neuromodulation, pain, hunger, panic, and fatigue are not merely philosophical anecdotes. They are physical and biological manipulations that alter conscious level, contents, selfhood, affect, agency, memory, and report. A theory that ignores this dependence is poorly matched to the evidence.

Second, materialism has causal discipline. If physical events in the body and brain are sufficient to drive behavior, then positing a separate nonphysical cause creates pressure from overdetermination or exclusion. Identity theory and functionalism offer different ways to avoid this: either conscious events are neural events, or mental states are physically realized causal roles. This does not solve every conceptual problem, but it explains why physicalism remains the default starting point in much scientific work.

Third, materialist theories generate manipulable research programs. NCC experiments, masking, binocular rivalry, no-report paradigms, TMS-EEG, perturbational complexity, anesthesia studies, and adversarial collaborations give investigators ways to test mechanisms. These methods do not settle phenomenality, but they are stronger than armchair metaphysics alone. The 2025 Cogitate adversarial work is especially important as a sign that rival theories can be forced into explicit predictions.

Fourth, the cluster is multi-scale rather than single-note. Philosophical materialism supplies ontology and bridge questions. Neurobiology supplies cellular, circuit, and network mechanisms. EM theories ask whether field-level variables have causal or identity significance. Computational and informational theories formalize architecture, control, learning, and possible multiple realization. Homeostatic and affective theories anchor consciousness in living regulation and value. A complete materialist theory may need several of these levels rather than only one.

Fifth, materialism handles ordinary clinical and comparative facts better than views that detach consciousness from the body. Brain injury can remove capacities. Anesthesia can switch experience off and on. Disorders of consciousness can leave behavior misleadingly absent while perturbational measures reveal residual complexity. Interoceptive and affective disruptions reshape experience. These facts do not prove reductive physicalism, but they make brain-and-body dependence a non-negotiable constraint.

The best version of the cluster is therefore not crude reductionism. It is a disciplined research posture: specify the target, name the bridge relation, give a mechanism, make risky predictions, and distinguish prediction from explanation.

## Serious Objections And Limits

The first limit is the hard-problem and explanatory-gap pressure. A materialist theory may explain discrimination, access, report, memory, attention, metacognition, global availability, bodily regulation, or action control and still leave phenomenal character underdescribed. This is why challenge arguments remain useful audits even though they are not positive mechanisms.

The second limit is correlation inflation. Neural, field, computational, and interoceptive markers can track consciousness without being identical with it. NCCs may be prerequisites, consequences, enablers, report conditions, or memory conditions rather than constitutive bases. The same caution applies to EM fields, information measures, criticality, free-energy measures, and allostatic variables.

The third limit is target drift. Materialist theories often use the same word, consciousness, for different explananda. Global workspace theories often excel at reportable access and flexible control. Recurrent processing may focus on local perceptual experience. Higher-order theories target metacognitive awareness. Homeostatic theories target felt bodily self and valence. EM theories often target binding and unity. Philosophical physicalism targets ontology. A theory can succeed on one target without succeeding on all.

The fourth limit is underdetermination. Several theories can reinterpret the same data. Front-parietal ignition, posterior hot zones, local recurrence, attention schema, higher-order monitoring, integrated information, critical dynamics, and predictive processing can overlap empirically. Without hard criteria and preregistered predictions, theories risk becoming too elastic.

The fifth limit is overbreadth. Functionalism, computation, information, homeostasis, free-energy minimization, and field integration can be defined so broadly that too many systems qualify. Thermostats regulate. The internet processes information. Seizures synchronize. Computers manipulate symbols. Brains always generate fields. None of those facts alone establishes a subject of experience.

The sixth limit is substrate uncertainty. Computational theories tend toward multiple realizability and possible AI consciousness. Biological and homeostatic theories tend to privilege living organisms. EM theories may require field-bearing implementation. Identity theories may require specific neural types. These disagreements matter for AI, uploading, animal consciousness, and clinical ethics.

The seventh limit is that physicalism itself needs content. If the physical means current physics, the thesis may be incomplete. If it means future ideal physics, the thesis can become too vague to explain. If it expands to include consciousness as a basic physical property, it may move toward Russellian or panpsychist territory rather than standard materialism.

## Comparison Axes For Theories In This Cluster

- Target phenomenon: phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, reportability, wakefulness, conscious contents, attention, metacognition, self-modeling, affect, interoception, agency, or AI consciousness.
- Proposed ontology: neural identity, physical realization, biological naturalism, computation, information dynamics, electromagnetic field pattern, homeostatic regulation, affective valuation, or eliminative revision.
- Bridge relation: identity, realization, constitution, causal enabling, grounding, correlation, prediction, reduction, emergence, organizational invariance, or illusion-style deflation.
- Evidence type: philosophical argument, causal-closure reasoning, lesion or stimulation evidence, anesthesia and sleep data, masking and rivalry, no-report paradigms, TMS-EEG, perturbational complexity, field measurements, computational modeling, AI indicators, interoceptive anatomy, affective neuroscience, and allostatic network evidence.
- Necessity and sufficiency: whether the proposed mechanism is necessary, sufficient, both, neither, or only a contributing background condition.
- Scope: ordinary adult human consciousness, infants, nonhuman animals, disorders of consciousness, artificial systems, altered states, emotional consciousness, or survival claims.
- Main risk: explaining access instead of phenomenality, treating correlates as causes, using formal elegance as confirmation, overgeneralizing from regulation or information, ignoring embodiment, or making a theory unfalsifiable.
- Hard test: whether a theory can predict changes in conscious level or contents under controlled interventions better than nearby rivals.

## Epistemic Status And What Not To Overstate

Mainstream scientific theory supports strong brain-and-body dependence for ordinary human consciousness. Lesions, stimulation, anesthesia, sleep, coma, seizures, drugs, neuromodulation, and disorders of consciousness are central constraints. Do not overstate this as proof that phenomenal consciousness has been reductively explained.

Active philosophical debate covers physicalism, identity theory, functionalism, eliminativism, illusionism, epiphenomenalism, the explanatory gap, Mary's room, bat subjectivity, zombies, multiple realizability, and the definition of the physical. Do not overstate philosophical popularity or professional default status as settlement.

Mainstream empirical research includes NCC work, global workspace models, recurrent and higher-order theories, clinical perturbation, no-report paradigms, and computational neuroscience. Do not infer that a successful predictor is automatically a constitutive explanation.

Scientifically interesting but less established territory includes EM field identity theories, some criticality theories, holonomic models, strong computational sufficiency, and some affect-first claims. These may become important, but they need clearer criteria and discriminating tests.

Speculative extensions include AI consciousness, substrate-independent consciousness, mind uploading, digital immortality, broad field-consciousness claims, and consciousness in generic self-organizing systems. These are not established by materialism alone.

Weakly evidenced or out-of-scope implications include survival beyond death, paranormal cognition, disembodied consciousness, or cosmic EM consciousness. This episode's sources do not support those claims.

Do not imply that neural correlates solve the hard problem. Do not imply that formal vocabulary is empirical confirmation. Do not imply that materialism is one theory. Do not imply that rejecting dualism automatically explains phenomenality. Do not imply that challenging materialism supplies a mechanism.

## Implications Only Where Sources Justify Them

AI consciousness: Computational and functionalist materialism make AI consciousness a live conceptual possibility, but not an established fact. Butlin et al.'s report is best used as a conditional indicator framework: if a theory is credible, then systems with the relevant architecture deserve further scrutiny. Current fluent language behavior is not enough. Homeostatic and biological theories make current AI less likely to qualify unless artificial systems acquire rich self-regulation, embodied agency, and perhaps affect-like control. EM theories would require attention to hardware field organization rather than software alone.

Virtual immortality and uploading: These sections do not establish uploading. Identity theory may tie consciousness to biological neural states. Functionalism and organizational invariance keep uploading conceptually open. EM theories may reject field-suppressed digital simulations. Homeostatic theories may require living or life-like regulation. Any uploading claim must specify both a consciousness criterion and a personal-identity criterion.

Survival beyond death: This materialist cluster supplies no positive evidence for disembodied survival. Brain-and-body dependence is a strong constraint against simple survival claims, though it does not by itself settle every metaphysical possibility. Survival claims would need independent evidence and a bridge relation explaining how consciousness persists without the relevant physical organization.

Meaning and value: Homeostatic and affective theories justify limited claims about value inside organismic life: pain, relief, threat, need, and care matter because they are felt by living subjects. They do not establish cosmic purpose, nihilism, or religious meaning. Materialism does not by itself settle value theory.

Clinical and ethical implications: Neurobiological measures such as perturbational complexity can matter for disorders of consciousness, anesthesia, and clinical assessment. But ethical conclusions require caution because measurement of conscious level is not identical to a full account of personhood, welfare, agency, or moral status.

Theory comparison: The most justified practical implication is audit discipline. Ask every theory what it explains, what it predicts, what would count against it, what relation links mechanism to experience, and what it merely redescribes.
