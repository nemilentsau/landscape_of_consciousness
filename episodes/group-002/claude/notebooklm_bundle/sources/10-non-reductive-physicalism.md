# Section 10: Non-reductive physicalism

## Kuhn Review Anchor
- Section: 10. Non-reductive physicalism
- Pages: 58-58
- Taxonomy placement: Non-reductive physicalism

## Research Question
Can consciousness be entirely physical while mental properties remain real, irreducible, and causally meaningful?

## Core Claim
Kuhn's section 10 presents non-reductive physicalism as a boundary position inside the physicalist family: consciousness is wholly generated by biological brains and involves no second substance, yet mental states or properties are not identical with, translatable into, or fully explained by physical states and laws. The view arose as a response to problems in early type-identity physicalism: Putnam's multiple-realizability challenge, Davidson's claim that intentional mental explanation is not governed by strict psychophysical laws, and Fodor's defense of the autonomy of the special sciences. In course terms, this is the hinge after section 9: materialism says consciousness is physical; non-reductive physicalism asks whether the physical can include higher-level mental reality without reducing it to neuroscience or physics. The most careful framing is not simply 'property dualism in disguise.' Some versions are predicate or explanatory dualism, where mental descriptions are indispensable but the tokens remain physical. Stronger versions treat mental properties as ontologically distinct higher-level properties realized by the physical, and those versions sit very close to property dualism, exactly as Kuhn notes.

## Strongest Case
The strongest case is that non-reductive physicalism preserves two commitments many philosophers and cognitive scientists find independently plausible. First, it respects physical dependence: ordinary human consciousness varies with the brain, and the view does not posit souls, disembodied minds, or violations of physical causation. Second, it protects the reality and autonomy of psychological explanation. Putnam's pain example makes the point vivid: if humans, dogs, birds, octopuses, and possible artificial or alien systems could all suffer, it is unlikely that pain as a mental kind is one neurochemical type. Fodor generalizes the same lesson to the special sciences: economics, biology, and psychology often use kinds and generalizations that crosscut microphysical taxonomies. Davidson adds a different route: the very features that make propositional attitudes mental, including rational interpretation and intentional content, resist strict lawlike reduction even if each mental event is also a physical event. For consciousness, the attraction is that the theory can say neural mechanisms are necessary and constitutive without pretending that first-person experience, intentional explanation, and higher-level mental categories disappear once a brain mechanism is named.

## Best Objections And Limits
The deepest objection is Jaegwon Kim's causal-exclusion problem. If every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, and if a mental property is distinct from its physical realizer, then the mental property seems either causally idle, systematically overdetermining behavior, or forced back into identity with the physical property. This is why Kim called non-reductive materialism unstable rather than a settled compromise. A second objection is that supervenience is too weak: saying there can be no mental difference without a physical difference may still be compatible with mental properties being genuinely nonphysical, so the view must specify realization, grounding, identity, or another dependence relation. A third objection is consciousness-specific: non-reductive physicalism may preserve the explanatory gap rather than close it, because saying experience is an irreducible higher-level physical property does not yet explain why those physical processes feel like anything. A fourth objection pushes from the other side: multiple realizability may be overstated, because neuroscience often finds shared mechanisms across species and because reductions can be local, structure-specific, or functional rather than one crude pain-equals-C-fiber identity. The resulting dilemma is pedagogically useful: if non-reductive physicalism makes mental properties too distinct, it looks like property dualism; if it makes them only conceptually or linguistically distinct, it may not be strong enough to explain phenomenal consciousness.

## Credibility / Epistemic Status
Epistemic status: active philosophical debate. The background claim that ordinary human consciousness depends deeply on functioning physical brains is part of mainstream scientific theory. The stronger non-reductive thesis, that mental properties are physically realized yet irreducible and causally/explanatorily autonomous, is a live but contested metaphysical position in philosophy of mind. It is not a fringe claim and not, in its general form, religious or spiritual metaphysics. Some later variants in Kuhn's section 10 family add theological commitments, but section 10 itself should be treated as secular analytic metaphysics continuous with debates about physicalism, realization, emergence, special-science explanation, and mental causation.
