## Research Question
Before arguing over a theory of consciousness, are we even using the word consciousness for one target or for several different targets?

## Core Claim
Kuhn's initial section is not a theory so much as a map legend. It argues that consciousness talk bundles together wakefulness, responsiveness, self-awareness, reportability, cognitive access, metacognition, emotion, phenomenal experience, and sometimes cosmic or nonmaterial significance. The decisive target for the hard problem is phenomenal consciousness: why there is something it is like to see, feel, think, or suffer at all. But scientific work often studies access consciousness, global broadcast, confidence monitoring, attention, report, and behavioral control because those are experimentally tractable. That mismatch is why Kuhn juxtaposes Block's phenomenal/access distinction, Dehaene-Lau-Kouider's C0/C1/C2 information-processing distinctions, Minsky's suitcase-term warning, Pigliucci's emotion point, Blackburn's Wittgensteinian deflation, and nonmaterialist challenges from Gomez-Marin and Davies. The section's practical claim for the course is methodological: every consciousness theory must first state which consciousness it explains and whether it is solving, reducing, dissolving, or relocating the hard problem and the related problem of mental causation.

## Strongest Case
The strongest case is that conceptual sorting is not pedantry; it prevents false victories. SEP's taxonomy and Block's 1995 paper show why explaining access, report, reasoning, and action can be different from explaining phenomenal character. Blindsight, masking, inattentional blindness, the split between unconscious processing and reportable perception, and no-report debates all make the distinction empirically useful: the brain can process information without the subject experiencing it in the ordinary way, and researchers need to know which contrast they are measuring. Dehaene, Lau, and Kouider make the functional side sharper by separating unconscious processing (C0), global availability for report and flexible use (C1), and self-monitoring or confidence (C2). Minsky's suitcase warning has methodological force: if consciousness names many processes, a search for the single place where consciousness is located may be misdesigned. The section also earns its breadth by showing why metaphysics remains in play: if phenomenal consciousness is not exhausted by access functions, then physicalism, panpsychism, dual-aspect monism, idealism, illusionism, and Wittgensteinian deflation are not merely competing answers but competing ways of framing the explanandum.

## Best Objections And Limits
The main objection is that splitting consciousness into phenomenal and access components may build in a controversial philosophy before the science starts. Cohen and Dennett argue that a science cannot confirm or falsify a consciousness detached from cognitive function and report; if a state can make no difference to report, memory, confidence, action, or measurement, it is unclear what empirical role it plays. Illusionists such as Frankish say the hard problem may be generated by how introspection represents our mental states, so the task is to explain why we judge that experience has private phenomenal properties. Blackburn's Wittgensteinian line raises a related worry: if consciousness is treated as a private inner object, we may manufacture the problems of other minds and private reference. There are also limits on Kuhn's broad opening frame. A suitcase diagnosis can become too deflationary, obscuring genuine unities among arousal, attention, integration, self-modeling, and report. Conversely, broad metaphysical alternatives such as panpsychism, idealism, or dual-aspect views may answer the explanatory gap only by adding ontology that has weak independent constraints. The result is a productive tension: the field needs clean distinctions, but too sharp a distinction between experience and function can make the target scientifically elusive.

## Credibility / Epistemic Status
Epistemic status: active philosophical debate. The distinctions between creature/state consciousness, phenomenal/access consciousness, report, attention, metacognition, and neural correlates are mainstream in philosophy of mind and cognitive neuroscience. Claims about global broadcast, metacognitive monitoring, and unconscious processing are parts of mainstream scientific theory, though not settled as full theories of phenomenal consciousness. The hard problem, private-language deflation, illusionism, and mental-causation worries are active philosophical debate. Kuhn's inclusion of panpsychism, idealism, dual-aspect monism, and other nonmaterialist proposals should be labeled speculative extension unless a later packet supplies a specific argument; religious or spiritual claims about survival or cosmic purpose are religious/spiritual metaphysics, and any empirical survival claim should be treated separately as a fringe/weakly evidenced claim unless supported by stronger source-specific evidence.
