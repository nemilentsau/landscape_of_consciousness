## Course Anchor

This episode covers Kuhn's top-level opening sections: Chalmers's hard problem of consciousness, initial thoughts, philosophical tensions, surveys and typologies, and opposing worldviews. The point of the episode is not to crown a theory. It frames the course's debate rules: specify the target sense of consciousness, distinguish phenomenal consciousness from access/report/function, avoid confusing correlation with causation or constitution, use surveys as maps rather than explanations, and disclose worldview commitments.

## Section Sources

### 1. Chalmers's hard problem of consciousness

Kuhn uses Chalmers's 1995 article and 1996 book as the watershed opening. The hard problem asks why physical or cognitive functions are accompanied by subjective experience. The strongest case comes from the gap between structure/function and felt quality, reinforced by Mary, zombies, Nagel's bat, and Levine's explanatory gap. The main objections are that conceivability may reflect concepts rather than metaphysical possibility, that Mary may gain an ability or first-person concept rather than a nonphysical fact, that young sciences often look explanatorily incomplete, and that naturalistic dualism has not supplied independently testable psychophysical laws.

### 2. Initial thoughts

Kuhn's second section acts as a map legend. "Consciousness" may refer to wakefulness, responsiveness, attention, access, report, self-awareness, metacognition, emotion, phenomenal experience, or broader metaphysical significance. Block's phenomenal/access distinction, Dehaene-Lau-Kouider's C0/C1/C2 framework, and Minsky's suitcase-term warning are central. The strongest case is that conceptual sorting prevents false victories. The main objection is that separating phenomenal consciousness too sharply from function and report may make it scientifically elusive.

### 3. Philosophical tensions

This section marks recurring slippages: epistemology versus ontology, correlation versus causation, and evidence versus metaphysical interpretation. Neural correlates of consciousness make the field tractable, but a correlate may be a prerequisite, consequence, report demand, attentional effect, or hidden common effect. No-report paradigms, lesion work, stimulation, causal modeling, and adversarial collaboration are attempts to tighten inference. The main limit is that the frame is general: many rival theories can accept the same warning and still disagree.

### 4. Surveys and typologies

Surveys and typologies are orientation tools, not explanations. Kuhn draws on Bayne, Chalmers's Type A-F taxonomy, PhilPapers categories, Seth and Bayne's neuroscience survey, explanatory-profile work, researcher surveys, and empirical-comparison databases. Their strongest contribution is turning theory proliferation into explicit questions about scope, ontology, mechanism, evidence, and testability. Their weakness is that maps inherit the priorities and biases of their designers.

### 5. Opposing worldviews

Kuhn frames major background commitments: physicalism/materialism, dualism, idealism, and surrounding hybrids. Physicalism has the strongest integration with neuroscience and causal closure. Dualism has pressure from phenomenology and the explanatory gap. Idealism has pressure from epistemic priority, because the physical world is known through experience. The frame is useful but coarse: it can hide nonreductive physicalism, neutral monism, Russellian monism, panpsychism, pluralism, and methodological naturalism.

## Production Notes
