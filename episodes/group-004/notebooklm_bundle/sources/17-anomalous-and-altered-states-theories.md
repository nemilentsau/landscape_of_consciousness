# Section 17: Anomalous and altered states theories

## Kuhn Review Anchor
- Section: 17. Anomalous and altered states theories
- Pages: 104-104
- Taxonomy placement: Anomalous and altered states theories

## Research Question
If ESP, out-of-body experience, or near-death experience ever became reliably veridical under controlled conditions, would consciousness have to be more than brain activity?

## Core Claim
Kuhn's section 17 is best treated as a boundary category rather than a single theory. It gathers anomalous and altered-state approaches that ask whether consciousness can access information, locations, persons, or realms that ordinary physicalist accounts say should be inaccessible: ESP, telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis, out-of-body experiences, near-death experiences, survival claims, and related altered states. The core theoretical move is conditional: if any robust psi or survival claim were established, then local brain-bound materialism would be under direct pressure and nonphysical, dualist, idealist, or nonlocal models would gain relevance. But the mere existence of unusual experiences does not by itself establish nonphysical consciousness. Altered states are real phenomena for consciousness science; the controversial leap is from 'people have vivid unusual experiences, and some experiments report small anomalies' to 'consciousness is nonphysical or can survive bodily death.' A serious treatment should separate psi from tabloid paranormalism, separate phenomenology from veridical evidence, and separate live anomalies from spiritual or metaphysical overreach.

## Strongest Case
The strongest case begins by noting that this field is not only anecdote. Psychical research and later parapsychology built controlled protocols precisely to reduce ordinary leakage, fraud, and sensory cueing. Proponents point to cumulative meta-analytic literatures: Bem and Honorton's ganzfeld review, Storm, Tressoldi, and Di Risio's later free-response meta-analysis, Bem's time-reversed psychology experiments, Utts's favorable assessment of parts of the U.S. remote-viewing literature, and Cardena's 2018 American Psychologist review of many classes of psi experiment. The steelman is not that every medium, psychic, NDE memoir, or paranormal story is true; it is that repeated small effects across independent designs, if not artifacts, would imply some anomalous information channel or mind-matter relation not captured by current neuroscience. Near-death and out-of-body literatures add a different pressure point: some people report vivid, life-changing experiences during severe physiological crisis, including apparent separation from the body and occasional claims of accurate perception while unconscious. Prospective cardiac-arrest studies by van Lommel and Parnia show that these reports can be studied clinically rather than only as folklore. Altered-state research also shows that selfhood, body ownership, time, salience, and world-modeling are much more plastic than everyday consciousness suggests. If even one tightly controlled case demonstrated information acquisition when normal sensory, inferential, and memory channels were excluded, Kuhn is right that the implications for theories of consciousness would be profound.

## Best Objections And Limits
The strongest objections are methodological, theoretical, and comparative. First, the prior probability is low because robust psi would conflict with deeply successful assumptions in physics, neuroscience, and psychology about causal locality, information transfer, and brain dependence. Low-prior claims require unusually clean, preregistered, independently replicated evidence, and the psi record is dominated by small effects, disputed meta-analyses, publication bias worries, optional stopping, flexible analysis, file-drawer concerns, and decline effects. Milton and Wiseman failed to replicate the ganzfeld effect claimed by Bem and Honorton; Wagenmakers and colleagues argued that Bem's apparent precognition evidence was weak or nonexistent under Bayesian analysis; Ritchie, Wiseman, and French failed to replicate a Bem experiment; and Rabeyron, a psi-sympathetic researcher, still frames psi as entangled with the broader replication crisis. Second, the 'experimenter effect' or psi paradox can protect the theory from falsification too easily: if failure to replicate is explained as psi avoiding replication, the claim loses scientific grip. Third, near-death and out-of-body experiences have serious naturalistic competitors. Hypoxia, hypercarbia, REM intrusion, seizures, drugs, memory reconstruction, expectation, trauma, and temporo-parietal multisensory disruption can produce many reported features. Blanke's stimulation work and Ehrsson's experimental OBE induction show that the felt location of the self can be experimentally manipulated. Fourth, prospective NDE studies are important but have not produced repeatable hidden-target confirmations of disembodied vision; striking cases remain sparse, retrospective, and hard to rule out by ordinary routes. Fifth, even a genuine anomaly would not automatically prove souls, spirits, afterlife realms, reincarnation, or idealism. It might instead require a narrower revision in cognition, statistics, physics, or experimental method.

## Credibility / Epistemic Status
Epistemic status: mainstream scientific theory for the existence and neural/psychological study of altered states, NDE reports, OBE-like illusions, hypnosis, meditation, psychedelics, and body-self plasticity; active philosophical debate for what anomalous experiences imply about selfhood, brain dependence, and the limits of third-person methods; speculative extension for psi-friendly nonlocal, dualist, idealist, or mind-matter interaction models; religious/spiritual metaphysics for claims about souls, spirits, survival after death, reincarnation, or nonphysical realms; fringe/weakly evidenced claim when anecdotal paranormal stories or contested small laboratory effects are presented as having already proved nonphysical consciousness. The balanced verdict is that anomalous reports are worth studying carefully, and some parapsychologists have used serious designs, but the current evidence does not justify treating psi or survival as established science. The nonphysical inference should remain conditional: revolutionary if veridical, unproven as of now.
