# Identity theory

## Core Claim
Kuhn uses classic mind-brain identity theory as the anchor, but his main move is broader. Classic identity theory says that mental states, events, or processes are identical with brain states, events, or processes, not merely caused by or correlated with them. Kuhn generalizes the point: any complete theory that does not treat consciousness as fundamental must eventually say what consciousness is. It may identify experience with a neural process, a functional role, an information structure, a biological organization, a representational state, or some other real feature of the world. The section is therefore less a defense of old type-identity theory than an audit rule for the rest of the map: correlation, enabling condition, causal precursor, and report mechanism are not yet an ontology of consciousness unless the theory specifies the identity claim it means to defend.

## Strongest Case
The strongest case is the familiar a posteriori identity strategy in science. Lightning turned out to be electrical discharge; water turned out to be H2O; heat was identified with molecular or statistical-physical processes in the relevant domain. Place, Smart, and Feigl argued that consciousness-brain identities could be empirical discoveries of the same broad kind, with different modes of presentation but one underlying referent. Lewis and Armstrong strengthened the case by tying mental concepts to causal roles: if the state that plays the pain role is in fact a neural or physical state, then pain is that state. Papineau-style physicalism adds pressure from causal closure: if conscious episodes make physical differences and physical effects already have sufficient physical causes, then treating conscious episodes as physical avoids systematic overdetermination. Kuhn's generalized version is especially useful pedagogically because it prevents weaker claims from passing as full explanations. A theory that only finds neural correlates, reports, attention markers, information measures, or computational functions still owes the listener a final answer: are those items the experience itself, or just things that accompany, enable, measure, or describe it?

## Best Objections
The classic objections still matter. Multiple realizability challenges strict type identity: pain, vision, or emotion may occur in humans, other mammals, birds, octopuses, hypothetical aliens, or machines without sharing one neurochemical type. Token identity can survive this, but it weakens the claim from one universal neural type to many local identities. Kripke's modal objection presses harder: if pain and a brain process are rigidly designated, an identity would be necessary if true, yet it seems conceivable that the brain process could occur without pain or pain without that exact process. Chalmers-style and Levine-style explanatory-gap worries add that even a perfect mapping from neural mechanisms to reports and discriminations may leave open why those mechanisms feel like anything from the first-person point of view. Neuroscience also gives a methodological caution: neural correlates of consciousness are often entangled with report, attention, memory, arousal, and task demands, so a correlation is not automatically an identity. Finally, Kuhn's generalization risks becoming too easy: if every complete non-fundamental theory is called an identity theory, the label gains reach but loses specificity unless the theory states the level, relata, and criteria by which the identity is being claimed.

## Credibility Notes
Epistemic status: active philosophical debate. The empirical claim that consciousness depends deeply on brain organization is strongly supported by mainstream neuroscience, anesthesia research, lesion studies, stimulation studies, sleep research, and neural-correlate work. The stronger metaphysical claim that conscious experience is identical with some physical, neural, functional, or informational state is not settled by those data alone. Classic type identity is historically central and still defensible in restricted or revised forms, but it is not the default mainstream scientific theory in its old pain equals one neural type form. Token physicalism, a posteriori physicalism, and local identity claims remain serious live options. Kuhn's broader claim that every non-fundamental complete theory must include an identity claim is best treated as a philosophical discipline for explanation, not as an empirical discovery. It is not a religious/spiritual metaphysics and not a fringe claim, but any announcement that science has already proved consciousness is identical to one specific mechanism should be marked as an overstatement.

## Listener Hooks
- Lightning lesson: identity theory asks whether consciousness and brain activity are two things correlated, or one thing described from two angles.
- The green afterimage: if nothing green is literally in the brain, what exactly is the green experience supposed to be?
- Pain across species: a human, a dog, an octopus, and a hypothetical Martian may all be in pain; does one physical type cover them all?
- Token versus type: maybe this particular toothache is identical with this neural event, even if toothache as a general kind has many realizations.
- The anesthesia switch: drugs can make the world vanish for a subject, but does that reveal the identity of consciousness or only one dependency condition?
- The theory invoice: every non-fundamental theory eventually has to pay by saying, plainly, consciousness is identical with what?
- Brain scan courtroom: a scan may predict what someone sees, but prediction and correlation are not yet metaphysical identity.
